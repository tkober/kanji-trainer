On'yomi — the "sound" reading

On'yomi (音読み) is the Chinese-derived reading a kanji picked up along with its meaning, roughly a thousand years ago. It is mostly used when the kanji appears inside a compound word made of two or more kanji (a jukugo), e.g. 月曜日 げつようび (Monday), where 月 contributes げつ, its on'yomi.

Dictionaries write on'yomi in katakana, and so does this app — tap or hover a converted reading anywhere in the item details to see the hiragana underneath, for whoever still struggles reading katakana at speed.

Kun'yomi — the native reading

Kun'yomi (訓読み) is the reading the word already had in Japanese before kanji arrived, attached to the character that matched its meaning. It is mostly used when the kanji stands alone or carries okurigana (kana endings that spell out inflection), e.g. 月 つき (moon) or 出る でる (to leave) — 出 alone, plus the okurigana る.

Dictionaries write kun'yomi in hiragana, and often mark with a dot where the okurigana begins (for example で・る), since the kanji itself only ever covers the stem.

Nanori — name readings

Nanori (名乗り) readings are used almost only in personal and place names. A kanji can have a nanori nobody would guess from its on'yomi or kun'yomi — which is exactly why names are read aloud when introduced, in Japan as much as anywhere kanji are used for names.

Why does one kanji have several readings?

Most kanji were borrowed for their meaning, not their sound, into a language that already had words for the same things. 月 meant "moon" in Japanese long before kanji existed, so it kept its native reading (つき) for that word and gained a borrowed one (げつ / がつ) for the compounds it came bundled with — two readings, two layers of history, one character to show for both.

Telling which one a word uses

Two rules of thumb cover most cases: a compound of two or more kanji with no okurigana is usually read by on'yomi throughout (月曜日 げつようび), while a single kanji standing alone or followed by okurigana is usually read by kun'yomi (月 つき, 出る でる).

Both rules have well-known exceptions. A jūbako reading mixes on'yomi first and kun'yomi second inside one compound (重箱 じゅうばこ, itself the term for this pattern); a yutō reading mixes them the other way round (湯桶 ゆとう). There is no shortcut around simply learning a compound's reading the way this app already drills it — the rules only help with a guess, never a certainty.

The kana convention used in this app

Following dictionary practice, on'yomi is shown in katakana and kun'yomi/nanori in hiragana — even though WaniKani itself stores and teaches every reading in hiragana. Tap or hover a katakana reading to see the hiragana spelling it is built from; typed answers are unaffected either way, since the review check folds katakana and hiragana together before comparing.

How readings are asked in reviews

A kanji taught by WaniKani usually has more than one reading recorded, but only one of them is ever asked for in a review or lesson quiz — the one WaniKani taught first. The others are shown dimmed, labelled "not asked in reviews": typing one of those in a review does not count as wrong, it comes back as a retry, because it genuinely is a reading of the character — just not the one the question meant.
